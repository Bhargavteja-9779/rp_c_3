LOOM helps most where faults increase leverage the most. Across datasets, its gain over PreVal under faults rises with the increase in leverage that the faults cause (Spearman ρ = {{f2(corr('gain_shift~lev_ratio_shift'))}}, p {{pv(corr('gain_shift~lev_ratio_shift','p'))}}, n = {{corr('gain_shift~lev_ratio_shift','n')}}), which is the mechanism we designed it for. The share of datasets on which LOOM beats PreVal {{trendclaim()}}. At the highest severity it is {{pct(winrate('noise',3),0)}} for noise, {{pct(winrate('spikes',3),0)}} for spikes, {{pct(winrate('missing',3),0)}} for dropouts, {{pct(winrate('wander',3),0)}} for baseline wander and {{pct(winrate('shift',3),0)}} for misalignment, but only {{pct(winrate('warp',3),0)}} for speed variation, whose median leverage ratio stays at {{f2(lev('warp3'))}}. A smooth change of speed of up to ±20 % leaves the pooled MiniRocket features largely where they were. Leverage then carries no signal, and LOOM behaves like an ordinary temperature-scaled ridge. Mild faults are also hard to tell apart from normal variation: at severity 1 the win rates of the other fault types are only {{sev1range()}}.

We found two ways in which LOOM fails. The first is *over-caution for faulty recordings that are still classified correctly*. The datasets on which LOOM loses most to PreVal under faults ({{worst('shift',0)}}, {{worst('shift',1)}} and {{worst('shift',2)}}) keep a high accuracy under faults (for example {{f2(worst('shift',2,'acc_shift'))}} for {{worst('shift',2)}}) while their leverage still grows ({{f1(worst('shift',2,'lev_ratio_shift'))}}× for {{worst('shift',2)}}). Lowering the confidence then costs likelihood. Leverage measures how unusual a recording is, not how likely the prediction is to be wrong, and across datasets the gain under faults is smaller where accuracy under faults stays high (ρ = {{f2(corr('gain_shift~acc_shift'))}}, p {{pv(corr('gain_shift~acc_shift','p'))}}). In a triage system this failure is relatively harmless: it sends a few correct cases to the reviewer.

The second is *many classes with very few training series each*. On clean data, the gain over PreVal shrinks as the number of classes grows (ρ = {{f2(corr('gain_clean~n_classes'))}}, p {{pv(corr('gain_clean~n_classes','p'))}}). The four largest clean losses occur on {{worstlist('clean',4)}}, which have only {{perclass_range('clean',4)}} training series per class on average. With so few examples, the smoothed targets give the true class only (n_k + 1)/(n_k + 2) of the probability mass, and the fitted model ends up underconfident. Smoothing is also what raises LOOM's clean ECE (Section 6.2). Without it, the median clean ECE is {{f(med('E3_ablation_clean','ece','LOOM-nosmooth'))}}, the same level as PreVal's {{f(med('E3_ablation_clean','ece','PreVal'))}}, but the NLL under faults is {{varword('E3_ablation_shifted','nll','LOOM-nosmooth')}} (Section 6.5).
