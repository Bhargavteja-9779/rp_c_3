**What leverage adds.** PreVal showed that exact LOO predictions turn ridge regression into a well-calibrated classifier at almost no cost [@dempster2025preval], and our clean-data results agree: the two cannot be told apart in NLL. They part ways when the recordings are damaged. A single scale factor keeps the confidence of a faulty recording at the level that suits clean data, and its NLL grows quickly (median {{f(med('E4_shifted','nll','PreVal'))}} over the fault conditions). LOOM uses a second quantity that the LOO algebra provides for free, the held-out leverage, to put each prediction on the scale of its own uncertainty. Its median NLL under faults is {{f(med('E4_shifted','nll','LOOM'))}}, and its confidence separates faulty from clean recordings better than any other method we tested, including QUANT (median AUROC {{f(med('E4_shifted','shift_auroc','LOOM'))}} vs {{f(med('E4_shifted','shift_auroc','QUANT'))}}). The ablation confirms that this comes from the leverage scaling and that the exact LOO pairs are needed to calibrate it.

**What this means in practice.** The application study translates these numbers into the terms that matter to an operator. With one recording in five sent to a human, LOOM's review queue contained a median of {{pct(cs('stream:fault_caught@20','LOOM'),0)}} of the damaged recordings, against {{pct(cs('stream:fault_caught@20','PreVal'),0)}} for PreVal, and fewer errors slipped through automatically. LOOM achieves this without extra models, extra data or a separate fault detector, with the same training cost as the ridge readout and on a standard CPU. This matters in settings where data are scarce and computing power is limited, which are common in wearable, clinical and industrial monitoring. LOOM is not a full replacement for a dedicated fault detector, however. It flags recordings that look unusual to the classifier, and it cannot tell a broken sensor from a genuinely new type of signal.

**How LOOM compares with other ways of being uncertain.** The behaviour resembles that of the distance-aware deep networks proposed to make neural networks uncertain away from their training data [@liu2020sngp; @vanamersfoort2020; @kristiadi2020]. For a linear readout on fixed random features, such distance awareness is available in closed form through the leverage, and it can be calibrated without held-out data. A nearest-neighbour distance used in the same role gave a similar NLL but detected faults worse. Leverage measures distance in the same regularised geometry that the classifier uses, and it comes from the decomposition that is computed anyway. QUANT, a tree ensemble on raw-series quantiles, reached a similar NLL under faults on average and a lower one under the strongest faults, at a higher clean NLL and about {{f1(eff('QUANT')/(eff('MiniRocket-transform') + eff('LOOM')))}} times LOOM's end-to-end training time. The two approaches have different strengths, and combining them is a promising direction.

**Which faults are covered.** Leverage grows when a fault pushes the features outside the range of the training data, as noise, spikes, dropouts, drift and misalignment do. A change in speed, or the switch to another sensor axis in Section 6.6, changes the signal while keeping the features in range. Leverage then stays close to its training value, and LOOM behaves like a globally calibrated model. Leverage scaling therefore complements, but does not replace, methods aimed at other kinds of change, such as new classes or changing class frequencies.

**Recommendations for practitioners.** We would use LOOM when (i) training sets are small, so that setting data aside for calibration hurts; (ii) recordings may be damaged or conditions may drift, as in most sensor applications; and (iii) a single fast model is preferred over an ensemble. If the test data are known to match the training data, especially with many classes and few examples per class, PreVal or LOOM without smoothing gives a lower clean calibration error. Where prediction sets are needed, jackknife+ with the LAC score gives the intended coverage with the smallest sets on clean data. If damaged inputs are expected, the APS score is the better choice, because LAC sets can become empty when LOOM flattens its probabilities, whereas APS sets grow instead, which is itself a warning sign (Table 4).

**Validity of the conclusions.** All conclusions are drawn across datasets with Wilcoxon tests and Holm's correction, and they hold on the datasets not used during development, without the datasets with train/test duplicates, and when related datasets are grouped (Section 6.6). They apply to random-kernel features with a ridge readout and to the faults we simulated. Section 8 lists further limitations.
